## Part 8. First six posts, drafted

### Post 1 (Pillar 1, hook post, publish first)
Most of what I read about AI agents hitting websites is written by people selling a detector.

I run security reliability engineering for a network operator, so I spent the last few months on the other side of it: what does an agent like Meta's Muse actually look like when it logs into a customer account and tries to cancel a service?

The short answer is that it looks like Chrome on Linux, from a CDN address, with no header, cookie or signature saying what it is. Every "obvious" check fails. The signals that survive are environment, egress, input artifacts and fleet behavior, and two of those will also flag screen-reader users and Private Relay customers if you are careless.

Over the next few weeks I will write about what we learned: why attribution has to be a layer you own, why the response should be observe, step up, then block last, and what you have to measure before you are allowed to block anything.

If you run a portal that customers log into, I'd like to hear what you are seeing.

### Post 2 (Pillar 2)
A firewall rule that silently stopped matching three months ago is an outage. We just don't call it one.

That sentence is most of the reason Cyber Security Reliability Engineering exists as a function. Security controls are production systems. They have owners, dependencies, error budgets and failure modes. The difference is that when they fail, nothing pages.

Three things we changed:
1. Every control gets a service objective that says what "working" means in a number.
2. A control that drifts from its objective opens an incident, with a blameless postmortem.
3. Toil gets measured and bought down like any other SRE team.

Security gets better when it stops being a department and becomes a reliability property of the platform.

### Post 3 (Pillar 3)
In January 2019 I joined Comcast to lead a team of one. Me.

The job was to build the capability to sell cyber security services to business customers. Twelve months later it was about thirty people across product engineering, production integration and a commercial SOC.

Things I'd tell anyone starting from zero inside a very large company:
- Spend the first ninety days with the people who already own the customer. You cannot build a service for a market you have not sat in a QBR with.
- Hire the integration lead before the product lead. The hard part is never the product.
- Write the operating model before the org chart. If you cannot describe the weekly loop, you do not have a team yet.

What did you learn building a function from nothing?

### Post 4 (Pillar 1)
The hardest customer to protect from your bot defenses is the one using a screen reader.

Agent detection leans on interaction signals: a mouse that teleports to the exact center of a button, clicks with no key events, a page that is read through the accessibility tree. Assistive technology produces some of those same signals for entirely human reasons.

If your false-positive budget is not set per cohort (assistive technology, Private Relay, VPN, enterprise proxy) and measured on real sessions before you enforce, you are going to block the people the law and basic decency say you must not block.

Measure first. Then step up to the customer's own device. Block last, narrowly, and explain why.

### Post 5 (Pillar 2)
If your AI agent's logs live only in the vendor's console, you do not have logs. You have a screenshot you have not taken yet.

Five requirements I now put on any agentic AI system before it touches production:
1. Telemetry in the OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions, not a proprietary format.
2. Every record carries the agent's registered identity and the human who kicked it off.
3. One trace ID from the first request through every model, retrieval and tool call, including MCP.
4. Telemetry goes to an enterprise collector, not just the developer tool.
5. Prompts and completions stored separately from security metadata, with secrets redacted before they leave the app.

None of this is exotic. All of it is missing from most deployments I have reviewed.

### Post 6 (Pillar 3)
I judged an internal innovation award this quarter and threw out my own scoring sheet halfway through.

The 40/30/30 rubric rewarded polished products. It could not see the difference between a team that inherited adoption (the tool was mandated) and one that earned it (people chose it over the thing they already had). It also could not see leverage: a product used by one team versus a platform three other teams built on.

Two questions fixed most of it:
- Who was the second user, and did anyone make them use it?
- What did someone else build on top of this that the original team did not plan?

Hidden gems are usually platforms nobody called platforms.
